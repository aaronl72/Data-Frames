Before you sign up

The 30-day clock starts at signup, not at first use. Everything here costs nothing and needs no account — do it first so day one isn't spent on setup.

Confirm the tooling works

# Azure CLI — this returned nothing when I checked, so verify it
az version

# If missing:
winget install -e --id Microsoft.AzureCLI

Decide the tenant question

Your position is a separate tenant per domain, on blast-radius-per-credential grounds. Relevant fact: data-frames.com is already a verified custom domain in an existing Microsoft 365 tenant — the MS=ms37433444 DNS record proves it. network-packets.com is not verified anywhere, so it's free to claim.

Signing up with a fresh email creates a new tenant with a default something.onmicrosoft.com name. You then add network-packets.com to it as a custom domain. Decide which email before you start — changing it later means starting over.

Confirm Hyper-V is available

Windows 11 Pro includes it, but it may not be turned on. Run as administrator, then reboot:

Enable-WindowsOptionalFeature -Online -FeatureName Microsoft-Hyper-V -All

Note that Hyper-V and VirtualBox historically fight over the hypervisor. If VirtualBox is installed and you need both, check that before committing to either.

Have the repo ready

The Bicep templates in infra/bicep/ deploy the network layer. They have never been compiled or run, so budget time on day one for fixing them rather than assuming they work.

The trial credit, and why it doesn't change the plan

A free account issues $200 USD of credit valid for 30 days. This plan spends roughly $20 of it and the rest expires. That is not a loss — it was never money you had.

The tempting argument is to spend the credit standing the full stack up in Azure so the templates get validated. It doesn't hold: the Bicep creates only virtual networks, subnets and NSGs, and none of those bill. Deploying the entire thing — including the lab VNet you won't populate for months — costs nothing at any time. You get complete template validation without the credit, without VMs, and without a migration at day 30.

The thirty days

Honeypot first — it's the only component whose value accrues in wall-clock time and cannot be compressed later.

DAY 1

Account, budget alert, and the whole network layer

Create the account and set a budget alert before deploying anything — Cost Management → Budgets, $50 with alerts at 50% and 90%. Two minutes, and it is the difference between a surprise and a warning.

az login
az account show --output table

az provider register --namespace Microsoft.Network
az provider register --namespace Microsoft.Compute
az provider register --namespace Microsoft.Storage

# Dry run first — expect errors, that is the point
az deployment sub what-if \
  --location canadacentral \
  --template-file infra/bicep/main.bicep \
  --parameters env=dev

# Then deploy the whole thing
az deployment sub create \
  --location canadacentral \
  --template-file infra/bicep/main.bicep \
  --parameters env=dev

Deploy both modules, including the lab VNet you will not populate for months. Virtual networks, subnets and NSGs do not bill, so the entire template validates for nothing — and the lab network sits there correct and waiting for the day you lift into it.

Quota is a non-issue on this plan: Cowrie needs one vCPU, comfortably inside any trial cap.

DAY 2–3

Honeypot up — the clock starts

Deploy Cowrie into snet-honeypot, not full T-Pot. Cowrie is the SSH and Telnet honeypot that produces the brute-force data your entire demo narrative rests on, it runs in 2 GB, and Wazuh already provides the dashboards T-Pot would have brought.

Configure the append-only SAS against the storage account, point network-packets.com at the public IP, then check the logs within the hour. A fresh public address is typically scanned within minutes — if nothing is arriving, the NSG is wrong. Treat silence as a misconfiguration, not as quiet.

This is the only irreversible timing decision on the page. Every day it is not running is a day of data you cannot retroactively buy.

~$20/month begins here
WEEK 1

Wazuh on Hyper-V — and the noise problem

All-in-one deployment locally, 12 GB while it is the only thing running. Take a checkpoint before the first configuration change, and another after every working state. This is the entire reason Wazuh is local: you will break it repeatedly, and a right-click revert takes thirty seconds.

Expect it to be extremely noisy out of the box. This is the real work of the month — decoders, rule syntax, and deciding what actually deserves an alert. Showing a prospect 40,000 alerts is worse than showing them twelve real ones.

Export every tuned rule to the repo as you go. The VM never migrates — the configuration does. Treat ossec.conf, custom rules and decoders as version-controlled from the first edit, and the eventual lift becomes a copy rather than a redo.

WEEK 2

Kali and targets — attacks you control

Kali plus a deliberately vulnerable target on the same host-only switch, with Wazuh agents on the targets. Now you can generate an attack on demand rather than waiting for the internet to supply one, which is what makes rule development iterative instead of observational.

The host-only switch has no route to the internet at all — a harder guarantee for R-03 than the NSG rule would have been. Nothing in this lab can reach a third party even by mistake.

WEEK 3

Connect the airlock, then build a playbook

Give the Wazuh VM a second NIC — one host-only facing the lab, one external restricted to Azure Storage. Every other VM stays host-only. Then point Wazuh at the blob container and pull. Two or three weeks of real attack data arrives at once.

Then Shuffle, locally, wired to Wazuh. Build one crude playbook end to end rather than a perfect one on paper: alert fires, enrich the source against AbuseIPDB, record it, notify you. Do not let this slip to week four. The temptation is to keep tuning Wazuh forever — but detection is a commodity and automated response is the differentiator.

WEEK 4

Tune against real traffic, and write it down

The honeypot now has a month behind it. Tune against genuine attack data rather than synthetic tests, get the dashboards to a state you would willingly show someone, and capture what the playbook does when it fires.

Record what broke — the Bicep fixes, the NSG rules that were wrong, the Wazuh rules you had to author. That record is the beginning of a client-deliverable methodology, and it is worth more than the lab itself.

TRIGGER

When to lift into Azure

Booked meetings, not a date on the calendar

The gap in this plan is narrow and specific: Shuffle only responds while the laptop is on, so you cannot show automated response with honest timestamps for an attack that happened overnight. Detection is unaffected and no data is ever lost — the airlock accumulates and Wazuh backfills.

That gap costs nothing until someone is watching. When prospect conversations are actually booked, lift Wazuh and Shuffle into the lab VNet that has been sitting deployed and empty since day one — roughly $150/month, from tuned configs already in the repo.

By then the honeypot data is months deep, so the demo is populated from the moment it goes up.

03

What will probably go wrong

Named in advance so they read as expected rather than as failure.

Hyper-V and VirtualBox fight over the hypervisor

If VirtualBox is installed, enabling Hyper-V can break it, and the failure is confusing — VMs simply refuse to start. Coexistence works on current versions but is not guaranteed.

Fix — check before enabling, not after

The second NIC on Wazuh is a hole you have to keep narrow

Wazuh needs outbound internet to pull the airlock, which breaks the otherwise complete host-only isolation. Configured loosely, it becomes general internet access for a VM sitting on the same switch as your vulnerable targets.

Fix — restrict it to Azure Storage, and keep every other VM host-only

The Bicep doesn't deploy first time

These templates have never been compiled or run. The most likely first failure is the AzurePlatformDNS and AzurePlatformIMDS service-tag rules — but that's a prediction, not a finding.

Fix — what-if before create, always

Wazuh's indexer won't start

The single most common all-in-one failure, and it's almost always memory. The indexer is a JVM and 4 GB is not enough regardless of what the minimum spec says.

Fix — B2ms with 8 GB, not B2s with 4

Nothing attacks the honeypot

If an hour passes with no inbound activity, the NSG is wrong or the public IP isn't associated. The internet is reliable about this — a fresh address gets scanned within minutes.

Fix — treat silence as a misconfiguration, not as quiet

You spend the whole month on Wazuh

Tuning is genuinely absorbing and there's always another rule. Week 3 arrives and Shuffle hasn't been touched — so the month produces a detection demo without the differentiator.

Fix — one working playbook by end of week 3, however crude
